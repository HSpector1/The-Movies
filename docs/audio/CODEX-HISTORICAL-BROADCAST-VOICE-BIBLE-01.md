# CODEX — Historical Broadcast Voice Bible 01

Status: **FIRST-PASS M6 DIRECTION — DOCUMENTATION ONLY**  
Date: 2026-08-31  
Purpose: historically grounded, non-imitative fictional Studio Radio direction from 1920 through 2040.

No historical voice may be cloned, impersonated, or used as guide audio. All example copy below is newly written Project: Studio material. This is a performance and writing bible—not authorization to record, cast, generate, or integrate voices.

All example station, sponsor, product, and service names are provisional fiction, not clearance results. Production use requires separate name, trademark, broadcaster/call-sign, and legal review; “newly written” does not make a name available.

## 1. Evidence law

- **SOURCE-DERIVED FINDING** identifies archival or contemporary evidence.
- **TECHNICAL INFERENCE** is a bounded interpretation of that evidence.
- **ARTISTIC INTERPRETATION** is an intentionally invented Studio Radio choice.
- **PROJECT: STUDIO RECOMMENDATION** is the proposed production direction.
- Pace ranges are **commissioning targets**, not claimed population averages, unless a source explicitly measured them.

Historical broadcasting was never one national voice. Network news, sponsored entertainment, local announcing, public affairs, community radio, music-format DJs, advertisements, and public-service announcements differed within the same year. Regional, Black, Latino, immigrant, noncommercial, and local broadcasters are not exceptions to be flattened into one “standard” accent.

## 2. Archive limits and method

**SOURCE-DERIVED FINDING:** Early broadcast survival is radically incomplete. Of roughly 500,000 broadcasts held by the Library of Congress, its 2016 account says only about fifty date from the 1920s; that is a statement about the Library's holdings, not an estimate of total U.S. survival. The NBC collection is far richer from the 1930s onward, while National Archives and American Archive of Public Broadcasting holdings add government, local, public, Black, and bilingual material. The result is a powerful but non-random sample—not a census of how Americans sounded. [Library of Congress Magazine, July/August 2016](https://www.loc.gov/lcm/pdf/LCM_2016_0708.pdf), [NBC Radio Collection](https://www.loc.gov/item/2002660093/), [National Archives radio guide](https://www.archives.gov/publications/ref-info-papers/70/part-4.html), [American Archive](https://americanarchive.org/)

This bible therefore:

1. studies grammar, pacing, role, and presentation across multiple institutional collections;
2. does not generalize a single famous network speaker into an era;
3. distinguishes contemporary measured guidance from inference;
4. specifies overlapping production practices rather than decade costumes;
5. prohibits direct transcript, slogan, voice, or signature-catchphrase reproduction.

This pass inspected institutional catalogues, finding aids, curatorial essays, available transcript records, and period professional manuals; it did not download or audition archival recordings. Some AAPB transcripts are machine-generated or unverified. Exact pitch range, timbre, pause duration, and audible treatment claims therefore remain deferred to a later authorized archive-listening study. Catalogue/transcript structure supports the writing grammar below, not voice imitation.

## 3. Performance comes before processing

### 3.1 Performance layer

Direct the actor and writer first:

- **rhythm:** clause timing, acceleration, rhetorical landing, and handoff to music;
- **breath grouping:** complete short thoughts rather than modern conversational run-ons where the format calls for formality;
- **diction:** intelligibility and intentional consonants without a theatrical “mid-Atlantic” mask;
- **vocabulary:** period-compatible business and entertainment language, screened for obsolete prejudice;
- **construction:** lead-first news, benefit-first advertising, conversational DJ links, or feature-style narration according to function;
- **energy:** credible purpose—urgent bulletin, warm companionship, hard sell, dry wit—not generalized “old-time excitement”;
- **social register:** national-network polish is only one option alongside local, community, public, music-format, and specialist voices.

### 3.2 Recording/presentation layer

Only after the dry performance works may presentation suggest technology:

- microphone distance/proximity and room contribution;
- mono or stereo image appropriate to the format;
- bandwidth and spectral balance;
- dynamics, saturation, transmission modulation, and noise floor;
- disc/transcription/tape/editing character where defensible;
- station ident, sting, bed, and music handoff conventions.

**Prohibition:** “Apply a radio filter” is not historical direction. Never use constant crackle, extreme telephone EQ, wow/flutter, or distortion to compensate for modern cadence and copywriting. Archive damage is not the sound of every original broadcast.

## 4. Pace and copy-length evidence

Two period professional sources show why one universal speed is untenable:

- A 1947 radio-news writing text reports WNEW tests favoring about **175 words per minute** for straight news.
- A 1957 broadcasting handbook gives about **100 words** for a one-minute commercial station announcement, up to **225 words per minute** for some commentators, roughly **140** for longer talks, and 110–135 for a named presidential example.

A modern reanalysis of the Boston University Radio News Corpus—seven professional FM announcers—reports about 5.31 syllables per second. It does not establish a universal words-per-minute range. These task- and language-dependent references are not instructions to mechanically time-stretch actors. [Warren, *Radio News Writing and Editing* (1947)](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/News/Radio-News-Writing-and-Editing-Warren-1947.pdf), [Abbott, *Handbook of Broadcasting*, 4th ed. (1957)](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Education/Handbook-of-Broadcasting-Abbot-4th-Edition-1957.pdf), [Frontiers FM-radio study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.869049/full)

**PROJECT: STUDIO RECOMMENDATION:** Commission to ranges below, then judge clarity and character by ear. Do not accelerate or pitch-shift with simulation speed.

## 6. Era profiles

### 6.1 `experimental_local_civic` — 1920–1926

**Source context:** Surviving 1920s material is sparse. WNYC's archive-curated 1924 launch record shows an explicitly civic/local mission. Commercial electrical examples existed by 1920; Columbia and Victor implemented and introduced the process at scale in 1925, while broadcast recording and network distribution continued developing. Do not make one preserved speech the national norm. [WNYC first broadcast](https://www.wnyc.org/story/wnycs-first-official-broadcast/), [Library of Congress recording timeline](https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-plan/tools-and-resources/historical-background/timeline/)

- **Presenter archetypes:** local station announcer/engineer; formal network announcer emerging; live entertainment master of ceremonies; direct product demonstrator.
- **Commissioning pace:** civic/ID 115–145 wpm; entertainment/ad 125–155. Treat wider variation as valid.
- **Diction/register:** deliberate, projected, clear call letters; full words; region may remain audible. No compulsory pseudo-British or universal transatlantic accent.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** compact declaratives, courtesy forms, explicit program and sponsor naming; one idea per sentence; occasional ceremonial introduction.
- **Humor/formality:** light wordplay in entertainment; news high formality; local hosts may be warmer and less polished.
- **Ad grammar:** product/sponsor first, concrete benefit, direct invitation, short proof or demonstration; avoid copied period slogans and medical claims.
- **News grammar:** identify source/place, state event, add one consequence; do not use theatrical urgency for routine lot news.
- **Station-ID grammar:** call sign + city/service + immediate program handoff.
- **Presentation:** dry mono, modest room, controlled bandwidth; technology may be suggested gently. Do not add continuous shellac crackle to live radio.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** machine-gun patter for every man; nasal vaudeville voice; “see here, old sport” filler; ethnic dialect comedy; fake static as history.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “This is Project Studio Radio, coming to you from the lot. The orchestra returns after the casting notice.”
  - News: “Stage Two is open at last. Camera crews report there at nine, and the west corridor will remain clear.”
  - Ad: “A clear call sheet saves a crowded morning. Ask the lot office for tomorrow's Project Planner before you leave.”

### 6.2 `network_formation_sponsor_transition` — 1926–1934

**Source context:** NBC's 1926 formation marks an institutional change, not an instant national vocal standard. A 1924 professional article already argued that the microphone rewarded economical, purposeful bridging language; network continuity and sponsor-program identity then grew alongside local presentation. [NBC Radio Collection guide](https://guides.loc.gov/nbc/radio-collections/broadcasts), [“Political Spellbinding by Radio” (1924)](https://earlyradiohistory.us/1924spl.htm)

- **Presenter archetypes:** precise network continuity announcer; personable local host; sponsor/program announcer; early news bulletin reader.
- **Commissioning pace:** news/continuity 125–160 wpm; local personality 120–155; advertising 125–165.
- **Diction/register:** correct names and call signs; clear affiliation; formal without grandeur; local pronunciation may remain.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** one or two clauses per continuity line; economical bridges; explicit origin/program/sponsor relationships.
- **Humor/formality:** light scripted entertainment humor; hard news remains direct and humorless.
- **Ad grammar:** sponsor presents program, or need → one benefit → invitation. Avoid relentless sponsor repetition.
- **News grammar:** bulletin label → source/time/place → verified fact → next update.
- **Station-ID grammar:** originating location → station/network relation → next program.
- **Presentation:** subtle local-room versus network-line contrast; mild bandwidth cues, not blanket telephone filtering.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** prestige-network diction as the only American speech; faux transatlantic costume; ceremonious uplift on every sentence.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “From Project Studio in Hollywood, the Continental Picture Hour now begins.”
  - News: “The rain has delayed the courthouse exterior. Interior work continues on Stage Five.”
  - Ad: “Brightwell Film Stock presents tonight's company report.”

### 6.3 `mature_network_wartime_service` — 1934–1945

**Source context:** A complete WJSV day from 1939 moves among formal sign-on, Arthur Godfrey's relaxed local personality lane, news, serial drama, advertising, sport, and comedy—direct evidence against one “Golden Age voice.” Library of Congress advertising research describes sponsor/agency influence; NBC/NARA holdings document network programs, news, wartime information, and public service. Bob Hope material shows sponsor references could be embedded through entertainment instead of isolated in a modern ad block. [LOC WJSV essay](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/WJSV.pdf), [LOC broadcast advertising guide](https://guides.loc.gov/consumer-advertising-great-depression/radio-and-broadcast-advertising), [Bob Hope and radio](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/bobhope/radio.html), [NARA WWII radio guide](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2022/08/23/a-users-guide-to-world-war-ii-era-radio-in-the-national-archives/)

- **Presenter archetypes:** network newsreader; sponsor announcer; variety MC; local civic/service announcer; wartime bulletin voice.
- **Commissioning pace:** long talk/continuity 130–155 wpm; news 150–175; urgent bulletin up to roughly 180; advertising 140–175.
- **Diction/register:** organized, forward, declarative; names and places carefully placed; entertainment warmer than news. Regional/local variation remains valid.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** lead fact first; parallel phrases; explicit transitions (“Meanwhile,” “On the lot”); sponsor copy favors benefit and assurance.
- **Humor/formality:** network news restrained; variety witty and sponsor-aware; wartime references handled soberly, never as nostalgic set dressing.
- **Ad grammar:** sponsor/program bond, problem → benefit → invitation; one memorable phrase maximum; fictional goods only.
- **News grammar:** dateline/source → event → immediate studio consequence. No presenter-created mechanics.
- **Station-ID grammar:** station/network/service, sponsor or program, concise time/continuity bridge.
- **Presentation:** mono; controlled proximity; modest band limitation; transcription/lacquer character only when the fiction calls for recorded playback. Noise stays below speech.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** universal booming “newsreel” voice; constant wartime rhetoric; anti-Communist parody outside historically and ethically reviewed content; gender/race/class stereotypes.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “Project Studio Radio, serving the lot and the picture colony. This hour is brought to you by Bright Arc Lamps.”
  - News: “The rail shipment reached the backlot before dawn. Construction may resume today; the western set remains closed until inspection.”
  - Ad: “When the scene runs late, Bright Arc keeps the close-up clear. Specify the new quiet housing at the equipment desk.”

### 6.4 `network_to_local_personality` — 1945–1955

**Source context:** A 1948 professional announcer handbook centers naturalness, vitality, friendliness, and adaptability. A surviving WNYC remote demonstrates first-person scene setting and natural correction. Bob and Ray's 1946 local work documents announcers improvising between records—personality delivery coexisting with formal network speech before Top 40 crystallized. [Henneke, *The Radio Announcer's Handbook* (1948)](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Business/Radio-Announcer's-Handbook-Henneke-1948.pdf), [AAPB WNYC remote, `cpb-aacip-526-ms3jw87t05`](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-526-ms3jw87t05), [Library of Congress Bob and Ray](https://blogs.loc.gov/now-see-hear/2020/12/bob-and-ray/)

- **Presenter archetypes:** network holdover newscaster; local music personality; sponsor/live-read announcer; community service voice.
- **Commissioning pace:** news 145–175; local personality 125–165; advertising 140–180.
- **Diction/register:** news remains crisp; personality links become more conversational, name-rich, and rhythmically varied; local accent need not be erased.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** shorter links, direct address, time/weather/lot context, setup → record/segment → callback.
- **Humor/formality:** dry ad-lib and character are welcome; news retains clear boundary and lower humor.
- **Ad grammar:** personality endorsement or concise announcer read; tangible benefit; no fake testimonial presented as fact.
- **News grammar:** headline cluster followed by one useful detail; distinguish bulletin from chatter with an ident/sting, not actor panic.
- **Station-ID grammar:** call sign + frequency/city + personality/program name + time-of-day handoff.
- **Presentation:** mono AM-inspired focus for music-format variants; close dynamic microphone; light compression as presentation color. Do not crush every role or add constant tuning noise.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** nonstop shouting, “atomic age” catchphrases, one rebellious male DJ as the whole era, compulsory crooner voice.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “Project Studio Radio—music for the morning crew across the lot.”
  - News: “A new soundstage opens at noon. The paint is dry, the lights are tested, and Production Three moves in after lunch.”
  - Ad: “Long day on the backlot? The Sunroom counter has coffee ready before the next cue.”

### 6.5 `am_personality_fm_divergence` — 1955–1967

**Source context:** Portable transistor listening and Top 40 helped make AM more personal for younger audiences, while FM offered a distinct higher-fidelity route. Smithsonian holdings from Black-oriented WANN and Spanish-language KCOR demonstrate substantial local identities outside a single mainstream voice. These lanes overlap rather than replacing one another. [Smithsonian transistor radio](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/object-project/online/household-hits/transistor-radio), [WANN “Deacon” microphone](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1190159), [KCOR history](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/earning-place-dial-raoul-cortez-kcor-and-spanish-language-radio)

- **Presenter archetypes:** restrained FM music curator; AM format personality; public-affairs host; local/community/bilingual announcer; specialist industry reporter.
- **Commissioning pace:** AM personality 155–190; news 150–180; FM arts 115–150; bilingual copy timed naturally in each language, not forced to identical word counts.
- **Diction/register:** broader authenticity range; intimate and informed voices can replace projection; code-switching and community pronunciation require native/local expertise.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** less ceremonial setup; topic knowledge and context; public-affairs questions; music links with concise credits and observations.
- **Humor/formality:** dry/observational on FM; energetic on AM; public affairs respectful and specific.
- **Ad grammar:** format-compatible short spot, underwriting-like restraint where fictionally appropriate, or energetic personality read. Never collapse all three.
- **News grammar:** headline → context → local consequence; interview clip setup may be implied in later production but no fake archival voice.
- **Station-ID grammar:** call sign/frequency/city plus format/service promise; bilingual ID variants where authentically cast and localized.
- **Presentation:** FM variants may be cleaner/wider and less aggressively voiced; AM remains narrower/denser; tape edit is permissible. The mix difference should be audible before added noise.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** every 1960s voice as a counterculture whisper; appropriated dialect; fake bilingual delivery; “groovy” tokenism.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “Project Studio FM—features, music, and the people who build the pictures.”
  - News: “The editing rooms are changing to a new tape system this week. Existing productions continue on their assigned equipment.”
  - Ad: “Tonight at the Commissary: a late menu for crews finishing after ten. Bring your lot badge.”

### 6.6 `fm_public_community_pluralization` — 1967–1980

**Source context:** The FCC's FM nonduplication initiative, WRVR's public-affairs/arts archive, and community stations including KBBF, KDNA, and Radio Bilingüe show widening practices. AAPB records also demonstrate transparent tape-replay framing and detailed time/service handoffs. Commercial AM formats continue; this is addition, not a clean replacement. [FCC 1965 Annual Report](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-308698A1.pdf), [AAPB WRVR exhibit](https://americanarchive.org/exhibits/wrvr), [AAPB Latino community radio](https://americanarchive.org/exhibits/latino-empowerment/2-origins-of-latino-community-radio), [KUNM 1968, `cpb-aacip-207-375tb5z2`](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-207-375tb5z2)

- **Presenter archetypes:** knowledgeable music host; local public-affairs presenter; concise format DJ; bilingual/community presenter; specialist industry reporter.
- **Commissioning pace:** general music format 140–180; fast Top 40 170–200; news/public affairs 145–175; long interview setup 120–155.
- **Diction/register:** conversational and subject-literate; community languages/accents cast authentically; recorded material described transparently.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** time → program/function → presenter; listener-relevant lead → attribution → concrete detail; direct public-service imperative plus reason.
- **Humor/formality:** observational and format-specific; public affairs respectful; no generic counterculture manner.
- **Ad grammar:** format-compatible spot or personality read; noncommercial support copy remains distinct from a hard sell.
- **News grammar:** lead → attribution → context/local consequence; introduce actuality and identify it on return.
- **Station-ID grammar:** station/service/frequency/time, with network or public-service credit only where useful.
- **Presentation:** clean FM stereo, mono/tape remotes, and processed AM may coexist. A field report need not sound like the studio host.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** universal hushed FM voice; tokenized accent; every 1970s voice as disco hype; fake bilingual delivery.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “It's seven-thirty at Project Studio. First, the overnight report; then Mara Chen on yesterday's rain delay.”
  - News: “Stage Six reopened at noon, according to the lot office. The afternoon company may report as called.”
  - Ad: “Support for the Studio Report comes from Harbor Payroll Services.”

### 6.7 `formatted_imaged_talk_satellite` — 1980–1996

**Source context:** Public, local, community, and commercial forms coexist. A 1987 announcing text describes roughly 150 wpm as typical news and roughly 200 as rapid while arguing that communication matters more than a “deep disembodied voice.” NPR completed nationwide satellite distribution in 1980, while a 1989 public-radio open layers network/member/frequency/underwriter/weather/time/host/headline grammar. AAPB also preserves 1990 local news magazines and long-running Black public affairs. [O'Donnell et al., *Announcing* (1987)](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Education/Announcing-Broadcast-Communicating-Today-O'Donnel-1987.pdf), [AAPB *Le Show* history](https://americanarchive.org/exhibits/le-show/3-brief-historical-overview-of-le-show), [AAPB *New Hampshire Daily*, `cpb-aacip-187-47rn8z7q`](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-187-47rn8z7q), [AAPB KQED Radio News, `cpb-aacip-526-sn00z7279k`](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-526-sn00z7279k), [AAPB *In Black America* preservation](https://blog.americanarchive.org/2019/05/13/twenty-three-years-of-in-black-america-will-be-restored-digitized-and-archived-for-research-and-future-generations/)

- **Presenter archetypes:** tightly formatted music DJ; conversational morning team; authoritative but less theatrical news anchor; public-radio host/reporter; community or bilingual specialist; industry trade update.
- **Commissioning pace:** imaging/promo 170–205; news 150–185; public/talk 125–170; advertisement according to format.
- **Diction/register:** clearer segmentation by audience and daypart; contractions and direct address normal; public/news roles prioritize precision; personality roles may overlap and interrupt naturally but remain intelligible.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** short headlines and teases; format clocks; reset the subject after breaks; later years permit compact conversational fragments without modern influencer filler.
- **Humor/formality:** personality-specific; ironic/dry delivery grows available but is not universal. News and sensitive milestones remain unforced.
- **Ad grammar:** hook → benefit → call to action; personality live-read variant; fictional legal copy kept intelligible; no recreated period brand cadence.
- **News grammar:** headline → tape/quote setup → context → lot effect; because Project: Studio will not fabricate archival actuality, voiced quotations require newly cast fictional speakers and explicit provenance.
- **Station-ID grammar:** concise branded positioner + call letters/service; recurring sonic logo may be short but must not repeat every link.
- **Presentation:** controlled compression/EQ, shorter edited segments, recurring but sparse imaging; 1990s variants may use clean digital edits. Preserve breath and dynamics; avoid “loudness-war” parody.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** wall-to-wall shock-jock aggression; every 1980s voice booming; every 1990s voice cynical; copied format slogans/jingles; appropriated urban slang; overproduced laser effects.
- **Original lines:**
  - ID: “Project Studio Radio—inside the business, around the lot.”
  - News: “Two pictures wrapped this afternoon. One moves to editing tomorrow; the other is waiting on a replacement effects shot.”
  - Ad: “Need a quiet hour between meetings? Reserve a booth at the Writers' Annex from any lot terminal.”

### 6.8 `consolidated_broadcast_early_digital` — 1996–2004

- **Evidence status:** NARA's technical history dates nonlinear computer sound editing to the mid-1990s. Applying that wider audio-production transition specifically to broadcast workflow is a **technical inference**; the Studio voice synthesis below is an **artistic interpretation**, not a claim that all stations converged at once.
- **Presenter archetypes:** formatted personality; call-in moderator; concise newsreader; public host; web-aware station producer.
- **Commissioning pace:** news 155–185; talk 140–190; compact link 155–205.
- **Diction/register:** natural contractions, directness, fewer ceremonial openings; credibility comes from specificity rather than announcer resonance.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** tease → fact → context → destination; web/on-demand references appear only when P13 says technology exists.
- **Humor/formality:** conversational; humor is character-based, not an era costume.
- **Ad/news/ID grammar:** shorter modular reads, clear separation between fictionally sponsored message and editorial bulletin; station identity survives but need not open every item.
- **Presentation:** clean digital edits, stereo beds, conservative processing, transcript/subtitle parity.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** generic shock-jock performance, copied format slogan, premature podcast framing, meaningless “dot-com” copy, automatic telephone-band filter.
- **Original line:** “Project Studio Radio. The finance desk closes at six; three productions remain on schedule, and the full report is available after this bulletin.”

### 6.9 `on_demand_broadcast_hybrid` — 2004–2029

- **Evidence status:** Library of Congress-supported scholarship places sustained podcast emergence in the mid-2000s, not the 1990s. Contemporary practice informs the remainder of this **Project interpretation**; no single delivery is presented as the period norm. [Library of Congress, *Saving New Sounds*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/21/00/88/18/2021008818/2021008818.pdf)
- **Presenter archetypes:** informed host; compact news explainer; specialist creator/business reporter; personable music curator; accessible text-first bulletin.
- **Commissioning pace:** 135–180 for most material; deliberate 120–150 alternative for accessibility; energized links up to 195 when intelligible.
- **Diction/register:** human, precise, low-theatre; varied accents and identities cast authentically; no mandatory vocal fry or faux intimacy.
- **Vocabulary/syntax:** headline and consequence first; short context; optional deeper segment; no filler banter required.
- **Humor/formality:** restrained, situational, opt-in. Serious milestones remain sincere.
- **Ad/news/ID grammar:** unmistakable sponsorship labels; modular bulletin; short station sonic mark used sparingly.
- **Presentation:** clean close mic, gentle room, platform-normalized loudness, subtitles/transcripts, separate voice control and ducking.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** “podcast voice,” endless host chemistry, fake authenticity, breathless influencer pitch, undisclosed native advertising.
- **Original line:** “You're listening to Project Studio. One production is blocked on a location permit; the lot desk has the next available step.”

### 6.10 `projected_legacy_service` — 2030–2040

- **Evidence status:** **ARTISTIC INTERPRETATION ONLY.** Future voice practice is unknowable.
- **Presenter archetype:** calm studio custodian who can shift between live-feeling bulletin, archival reflection, and concise operational handoff.
- **Commissioning pace:** 130–175, with user-selectable clarity/pace variants where production permits.
- **Diction/vocabulary/syntax:** plain, specific, respectful; one idea per sentence; never perform “the future” through robotic affect.
- **Humor/formality:** gentle institutional memory, minimal snark.
- **Ad/news/ID grammar:** advertising may be disabled; bulletins cite source/context; ident recombines earlier station cadence without quoting it.
- **Presentation:** transparent, accessible, multi-language ready; historical treatments used only when a fictional archive item is clearly framed.
- **Prohibited caricatures:** AI assistant voice, vocoder futurism, omniscient narrator, techno-jargon, synthetic clone of any earlier presenter.
- **Original line:** “Project Studio Radio. A century of pictures lives on this lot; today's crews are writing the next reel.”

## 7. Cross-role grammar

| Function | Required rhetorical order | Typical length target | Music/ident relationship |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Operational PA | condition → location/production → requested action | 5–14 seconds | No jingle; only a short priority tone if accessibility testing supports it. |
| Current news | headline → context → externally supplied lot consequence | 12–35 seconds | Short bed optional; never masks key nouns/numbers. |
| Future/industry outlook | explicit uncertainty/source → expected change → timing | 15–40 seconds | Must not assert uncommitted P13 truth; no sensational sting. |
| DJ link | identify/reflect → brief studio color → music handoff | 7–25 seconds | Variation pool and cooldown; never between every track. |
| Advertisement | sponsor disclosure → need → fictional benefit → action | 10–30 seconds | Distinct but quiet branding; transcript exactly matches material meaning. |
| Station ID | service identity → location/format promise | 2–8 seconds | Sparse sonic mark; variants prevent repetition. |
| Public-service item | issue → safe action/source → concise close | 10–30 seconds | No comedy or fabricated authority. |

## 8. M6 production guardrails

- Voice may report only P13/TypeScript-published truth. Presentation randomness cannot invent events or outcomes.
- Every line requires text, subtitle, localization context, pronunciation notes, content tags, speaker role, era-validity range, and provenance.
- Provide independent radio, PA, dialogue, music, ambience, and UI volume controls; captions/transcripts remain available when audio is muted.
- Duck music gently for optional radio; operational PA may use stronger priority. Never stack DJ, bulletin, PA, and milestone sting.
- Use minimum dwell/cooldown and per-session/per-campaign repetition counters. Silence is valid.
- Cast people, not stereotypes. Accent is not a substitute for period writing. Cultural/community roles need qualified consultants and performers.
- No historical voice cloning, imitation, transcript reconstruction, or guide-audio ingestion.
- Recording treatment must be defeatable or reduced for clarity/accessibility. Preserve a clean master.
- Do not pitch or time-compress voices at 2×/4× simulation speed.
