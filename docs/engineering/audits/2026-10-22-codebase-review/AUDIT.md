## A. Executive assessment

**What is strongest.** The simulation core is a pure, seeded, insertion-ordered state machine with one random-stream consumer per tick, derived streams keyed by permanent ids, and fail-closed validation at every boundary (tick entry, every action batch, every save). No reviewer found a determinism defect, a double settlement, a duplicated or lost dollar in the player ledger, or a path where the UI or the Bridge mutates authoritative state outside the core. The persistence layer validates every era on load, refuses lossy downgrades, round-trips bytes, and recomputes history from facts. The Bridge keeps TypeScript as the only authority, revalidates every quote at commit, serializes through one coordinator with an atomic fsynced store, and generates its C# contract deterministically. The newer tooling (setup helper, capture and probe scripts, sha-pinned genuine fixtures) is careful. The architectural direction is suitable to continue on.

**What is most likely to cause serious gameplay defects.** The same validators that protect the state also brick it. Eleven landed defects each turn an ordinary sequence into a state that cannot tick or save: a writer whose contract expires during a multi-week draft (L-01), an auditioned actor who later changes profession (L-02), payroll written as two ledger rows against an exact floating-point cash check (L-03), a person seated by both the player and a rival (L-04), a closed rival's frozen work still claiming released people (L-05), three cancellation-and-restart journeys in facilities and technology (L-06 to L-08). They share one root cause: the laws for "who is busy", "how long must a contract run", "which installation records are live" and "which profession counts" each exist as several private copies, and some validators judge historical records against today's facts. None of these paths has a test.

**What threatens integration and future development.** Three things. First, the P16 candidate inherits and extends the busy-set-versus-validator conflict into absorbed work (C-01), so landing it as-is makes a sanctioned write-off absorption produce an unsaveable world. Second, the P16 evidence does not describe the candidate: nothing has executed on current200, seventeen of its test files have never run and roughly sixteen of their tests fail by static reading, the generation runner the handoff names would stop at its own admission check on a stale pin, and thirty-one tests depend on captures outside the repository (C-03 to C-06). Third, the Bridge contract has no proven Unity consumer beyond projection 11, and its per-command cost grows with campaign age toward a hard 64 MiB journal cliff (L-11, L-12). Behind those, the P17 and P18 drafts cannot be rebased mechanically, and P18 needs a P16 rights-model amendment that must be decided before Save49 freezes (C-07).

**What remains genuinely unverified.** Everything at runtime. CI has been dark since 2026-10-01 because the repository-hygiene step crashes on the eight committed skill symlinks and false-flags 185 committed evidence files (L-13). The last recorded broad core run had 162 failures by design and took 6,425 s of wall time on this machine. The lane-guard hook is a partial backstop with real regex gaps. Timing budgets are enforced only by the external lane recorder. Several soft-lock findings are proved as law conflicts but their natural reachability needs one run each (named in Section D).

**Direction.** Continue on this architecture. Do not add P16 surfaces on top of the conflicts above. The repair sequence in Section F front-loads the four shared-law fixes and the P16 evidence rebind, because every later slice depends on them.

**L-01 · P1 · A multi-week draft can outlive its writer's contract; the invariant then blocks ticks, actions and saves.** (PROD-01 ≡ PEOPLE-02, two independent reviewers.)
`src/core/actions.ts:2099-2195` (original commission; `draftWeeks` 1–6 set at 2148-2153 with no contract-end check; zero `endWeekExclusive` references in 2099-2245), `:2202-2245` (pooling), `productionAdmission.ts:246-265` (`requireCommissionableWriter` checks only contracted-now and idle), `scriptDevelopment.ts:780` (due week), `tick.ts:275` (step 0.5 completes due writing) then `tick.ts:1049` (expires contracts regardless), `scriptDevelopment.ts:1115-1118` (invariant: every active writer must be contracted). The rival path has the guard (`hollywoodTick.ts:359`). The contract document's safety argument assumed one-week drafts (`docs/SCRIPT-PROJECTS-V1-CONTRACT.md:96-97`); C2a-M3 made originals multi-week.

**L-03 · P1 · Payroll is debited once but written as two ledger rows, against an exact floating-point cash equality.** (CORE-01.)

**L-12 · P1 · Per-dispatch Bridge cost grows with save and journal size toward a hard cliff.** (BRIDGE-02; same cost family as SAVE-03, BRIDGE-06, CORE debt 1, UI-14.)
`bridge/session.ts:2295-2333`, `runtime-checkpoint.ts:490-512, 1124-1150, 223-230`, `runtime-coordinator.ts:129-134`, `campaign-storage-codec.ts:20-36`: every journaled dispatch rebuilds the journal, re-imports and re-exports both save slots, JSON-parses and schema-walks every journal response (each a full snapshot), re-encodes a ≤192 MiB checkpoint, gzips, and rewrites and fsyncs the whole ≤256 MiB library; `executeCommand` dry-runs every intent and applies the chosen one again. R05 measured a 49.6 MB save and 133.5 MB checkpoint at week 8791; startup took 17.7 s; Unity times out at 5 s POST / 3 s GET. A 64 MiB journal holds about one Save response at that scale, so roughly every second Save rolls the session over (SESSION_MISMATCH) and Unity discards the pending action; a save over 64 MiB poisons the runtime on every Save.
