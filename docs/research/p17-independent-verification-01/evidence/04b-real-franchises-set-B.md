# 04b — Real Franchise Case Studies, Set B

P17 evidence file. Read-only web research, 2026-09-11; verification pass 2026-09-12 re-fetched every figure previously marked "(memory, unverified)" (all now sourced except one RT score, see §4). No repository access was needed or used.

Cases: Planet of the Apes (1968–73 cadence; 2001 remake vs 2011 reboot; 2024 continuation), Rocky/Creed, Mad Max/Furiosa, Terminator, Ghostbusters, Jurassic Park/World, Expendables, plus celebrity/non-actor evidence (Space Jam, Kazaam, 8 Mile, Trainwreck, A Star Is Born, Dunkirk, athlete cameos, Space Jam: A New Legacy).

Every case is used only to answer design questions A–I in section 2. Section 1 is OBSERVED HISTORY (dated, sourced). Section 2 separates OBSERVED HISTORY from DESIGN INFERENCE line by line. Section 3 is the summary table. Section 4 lists data caveats and open gaps.

Source-line format under every major claim: `> source; locator; what it proves; confidence; tier`.

Number conventions: all grosses are nominal USD, unadjusted for inflation unless stated. "Opening" = domestic 3-day opening weekend unless stated. Month gaps are computed from the release dates quoted in the tables (US wide release where two dates exist). The Numbers and Box Office Mojo occasionally differ by 1–3% on totals (different cut-off dates / re-release inclusion); where they differ both are shown or the difference is noted.

## 0. Method and source tiers

- Primary numeric sources: The Numbers franchise pages (fetched directly), Box Office Mojo franchise/release pages (fetched or via search excerpt), Wikipedia film/franchise articles (which cite BOM/The Numbers). Tier used: **OBSERVED HISTORY**.
- Trade press (Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Deadline, TheWrap, Forbes) for loss estimates, opening-weekend analyses, and stated causes. Tier: **CONTEMPORARY PROFESSIONAL SOURCE** (loss estimates are trade-press models, not audited studio accounts; treated as MEDIUM confidence).
- Rotten Tomatoes / CinemaScore quoted from the above sources. Where a score is from memory and was not re-verified in this pass, it is marked "(memory, unverified)" and no inference rests on it.
- Design inferences are labelled **DESIGN INFERENCE** and never presented as history.

Opening-weekend audiences graded all four post-T2 entries identically (CinemaScore B+) whether critics gave 70% or 26% — the people who showed up on opening weekend were the same loyal core each time; what shrank was everyone else.

## 2. Answers to the design questions

Format per question: OBSERVED HISTORY data points (dated, sourced above) → DESIGN INFERENCE → safe rule → must-not-over-generalize.

### A. Can rapid follow-ups capitalize on momentum?

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Planet of the Apes 1970→1971→1972→1973, gaps 12/13/12 months: each sequel was profitable on a shrinking budget, but domestic gross fell every year ($17.5M → $12.3M → $9.7M → $8.8M). Annual cadence extracted five profitable films from one hit but on a steadily declining curve. [HIGH; OBSERVED HISTORY, §1.1]
2. Expendables 2010→2012→2014, gaps 24/24 months: E2 grew worldwide (+16%, $312M) while shrinking domestically (−18% opening); E3 fell hard (−44% opening, −33% WW). Two-year cadence captured one more up-cycle, then collapsed. [HIGH; §1.7]
3. Ghostbusters Afterlife→Frozen Empire, 28 months: opening flat ($44.0M → $45.0M), worldwide flat ($203M → $200M), on a higher budget ($75M → $100M) with weaker reviews; the fast follow-up neither gained nor lost audience, it lost margin. [HIGH; §1.5]
4. Jurassic World→Fallen Kingdom, 36 months after a $1.67B record: $1.31B (−22%) on a lower budget — the fastest follow-up in the modern series was still the second-most-profitable entry. Apes Rise→Dawn, 35 months: opening +32% ($54.8M → $72.6M), WW +51% ($471M → $711M), the series peak. [HIGH; §1.6, §1.1]

DESIGN INFERENCE: A fast follow-up to a genuinely well-received hit (Rise→Dawn, JW→FK) captured the momentum window; a fast follow-up to a mediocre or gimmick-based hit (Apes 1970s annual run, Expendables 2→3, Afterlife→Frozen Empire) converted momentum into fatigue within one or two entries. Cadence is a multiplier on the predecessor's reception, not an independent good or bad.

Safe rule: Momentum from a well-received predecessor should be spendable within ~1–3 years and should raise opening-reach expectation; the same speed after a mediocre predecessor should add to Fatigue rather than Momentum. (Consistent with owner direction C/D.)

Must NOT over-generalize: The Apes 1970s run was also driven by Fox's cash crisis (budgets cut regardless of returns) and was *profitable every year* — "declining gross" ≠ "bad bet"; the game must not treat a declining-but-profitable annual cadence as a failure state.

### B. When does volume create fatigue? (quality vs cadence)

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Apes 1968–73: the best-reviewed sequel (Escape, 77%) grossed less than the worse-reviewed one before it (Beneath, 37%) — a quality bump did not reverse decline under annual cadence with shrinking spectacle budgets. Decline rates softened as the series went on (−46%, −30%, −21%, −9%), i.e., a floor of loyal audience remained. [HIGH; §1.1]
2. Terminator 2003–2019 (gaps 71/73/52 months): openings held $27–44M but domestic totals fell every time; reviews were 70/33/26/70 — the decline continued through the well-reviewed entry (Dark Fate), so accumulated distrust from Salvation/Genisys, not the current film's quality, drove the final collapse. [HIGH; §1.4]
3. Jurassic World 2015–2025 (gaps 36/48/37 months): $1.67B → $1.31B → $1.00B → $0.87B with reviews 71→47→29→53; the recovery in reviews (Rebirth 53%) did not stop the decline; the audience score for Dominion (79%) was fine even as its critics' score was the franchise low. [HIGH for numbers; §1.6]
4. Expendables: adding more famous names each time (E2, E3) coincided with each decline; the ensemble's novelty depreciated at 2-year cadence. [HIGH; §1.7]

DESIGN INFERENCE: Fatigue is cumulative and lagging: it is built by the *previous* entries' mediocrity and is paid by the *current* entry regardless of its own quality. Cadence controls how much fatigue accumulates per unit of calendar time; quality controls the sign of each increment. Recognition (openings) persists long after Momentum has gone negative — the Terminator band of $27–44M openings against collapsing legs is the cleanest demonstration that these are separate variables.

Safe rule: Fatigue should rise per installment weighted by (mediocrity of that installment) and decay slowly with time; a single good entry after several mediocre ones should reduce Fatigue only partially, not reset it. Recognition should barely move on a flop.

Must NOT over-generalize: Do not model fatigue as a function of count or cadence alone — Apes 2011–2017 (three films in six years, all well-reviewed) grew then held; MCU-style dense cadence with sustained quality did not fatigue for a decade. Owner direction C ("repeated mediocre output raises it faster than repeated excellent output") matches the evidence.

### C. Can one weak installment fail to kill a powerful franchise?

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Rocky V (1990): −68% domestic, 32% RT, budget up to $42M. Franchise dormant 16 years, then Rocky Balboa (2006) earned 6.5x its $24M budget, and the Creed spin-off (2015–2023) set franchise-record openings. [HIGH; §1.2]
2. Jurassic Park III (2001): $366M WW, ~49% RT, series-low. 14 years later Jurassic World opened at $208.8M and grossed $1.67B. [HIGH; §1.6]
3. Ghostbusters (2016): "$70M loss", sequel abandoned. Five years later Afterlife matched the opening ($44M vs $46M) on half the budget and turned a profit; Recognition was intact. [HIGH numbers / MEDIUM loss figure; §1.5]
4. Terminator Salvation (2009, 33% RT, trilogy scrapped) → Genisys (2015) still opened $27M and grossed $432M WW (China-led) — the property survived one bad entry; it was the *second and third* mediocre entries that destroyed legs and financing willingness. [HIGH; §1.4]
5. Counter-case: Beneath (1970, 37%) did not end the Apes series — but Battle (1973, 33%) did, because it was the fifth entry in five years; the series' end was pre-planned as a budget decision, not an audience verdict. [HIGH; §1.1]

DESIGN INFERENCE: One weak installment lowers Momentum sharply and raises Fatigue moderately; Recognition survives. Recovery historically came with either (a) dormancy plus a deliberately smaller, better-reviewed entry (Rocky Balboa, Afterlife) or (b) a much bigger, differently-positioned entry after a long gap (Jurassic World). The killers were consecutive weak entries at medium cadence (Terminator ×3).

Safe rule: A single flop must never make a franchise unusable (owner direction P); it should push the franchise toward COOLING/DORMANT with Momentum negative, Fatigue elevated, and financing willingness reduced — all recoverable.

Must NOT over-generalize: Recovery was never automatic and never happened on the *same* approach — every recovery changed something (scale, type, cast positioning, or gap). The sim should not grant a "bounce-back bonus" merely for waiting.

### D. How do successful spin-offs inherit recognition?

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Creed (2015): opened $29.6M (3-day), 2.4x the parent's last entry (Rocky Balboa $12.2M, 2006), on a $35M budget; positioned as a character spin-off with the parent's iconic lead in a supporting role (Stallone, Oscar nomination). [HIGH; §1.2]
2. Creed III (2023): $58.4M opening, franchise record in nominal terms, without the parent's lead on screen; RT 89%. Spin-off outgrew parent in nominal openings and domestic totals (inflation caveat: roughly equal to Rocky IV's opening in real terms). [HIGH; §1.2]
3. Furiosa (2024): prequel spin-off from Fury Road ($380M WW, 97% RT, 9 years earlier) opened $26.3M vs Fury Road's $45.4M (−42%), $174M WW (−54%), ~$120M loss despite 90% RT; the title character was recast and the parent's title character (Max) was absent. [HIGH numbers / MEDIUM loss; §1.3]
4. Space Jam: A New Legacy (2021) — arguably a soft spin-off/reboot around a new star: opening $31.1M beat forecasts and the original's nominal opening; 25% RT; legs weak ($70.6M domestic on $150M, day-and-date streaming). [HIGH; §1.8]

DESIGN INFERENCE: A spin-off inherits *Recognition* (opening-reach) at a discount that depends on how central the SubProperty is and whether a franchise-important association bridges it (Stallone in Creed = bridge; Theron/Hardy absent from Furiosa = no bridge). Whether it then *outgrows* the parent depends on its own reception building a new Momentum stream on the branch. Furiosa shows that a spin-off can inherit less than half of the parent's opening when the parent's own theatrical reach was modest and the bridging associations are missing.

Safe rule: SubProperty spin-offs start with a fraction of parent Recognition (weighted by SubProperty importance) plus a bonus for each franchise-important talent association carried over; their Momentum starts near neutral and is earned by their own reception.

Must NOT over-generalize: Creed's growth took three films and eight years; the game must not let a spin-off exceed the parent on its first entry by default, nor assume a critically acclaimed parent (Fury Road, 97%) guarantees a spin-off audience.

### E. How do dormant franchises revive?

OBSERVED HISTORY (gap → what carried recognition → outcome):
1. Rocky Balboa, 193 months (16 yr): iconic lead who was also writer/director; nostalgia; small budget ($24M) → $156M WW, 78% RT. [HIGH; §1.2]
2. Terminator 3, 144 months (12 yr): iconic lead (Schwarzenegger) without the signature director → franchise-record opening $44M, $433M WW, 70% RT. [HIGH; §1.4]
3. Jurassic World, 167 months (14 yr): StoryProperty + brand imagery + Spielberg as EP + nostalgia marketing; essentially no returning cast (B.D. Wong only) → $208.8M opening (4x prior franchise record), $1.67B WW. [HIGH; §1.6]
4. Mad Max: Fury Road, 358 months (30 yr): signature director (Miller) + StoryProperty; lead recast → $45.4M opening, $380M WW, 97% RT, 6 Oscars; theatrical profit marginal. [HIGH; §1.3]
5. Ghostbusters (2016), 325 months (27 yr): StoryProperty + new cast + original-cast cameos as *different* characters → $46M opening, $229M WW, reported loss, no continuation. Afterlife (2021), 64 months after that, legacy sequel with original cast in support → $44M opening, profitable. [HIGH; §1.5]
6. Planet of the Apes (2001), 337 months: remake with new cast → $68.5M opening, $362M WW, no continuation; Rise (2011), 120 months later: reboot → $54.8M opening, four-film branch. [HIGH; §1.1]

DESIGN INFERENCE: Dormancy did not depress openings in any case in this set; four of six revivals opened at or above the franchise's previous record. Recognition is durable across 12–30 years. What the revival *did* with that opening (legs, profit, whether a branch formed) was decided by reception and budget. The "carrier" varied: iconic lead (Rocky, T3), signature director (Fury Road), StoryProperty/brand alone (Jurassic World, Ghostbusters 2016).

Safe rule: Recognition decays very slowly; a REVIVAL CANDIDATE should be able to open on par with historical peaks (scaled to the current market) if at least one franchise-important association or the StoryProperty's defining hit is invoked; nostalgia is a marketing-efficiency bonus on the opening, not a quality bonus.

Must NOT over-generalize: Dormancy is not itself a bonus — Furiosa (9-year gap) and Expend4bles (9-year gap) both collapsed. The gap only matters in that it (a) prevents fatigue accumulation and (b) lets nostalgia marketing work; it does not restore Momentum.

### F. What makes a reboot different from a remake? (concrete pair)

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Planet of the Apes (2001) — REMAKE / "re-imagining" of the 1968 film (same premise: astronaut crash-lands on ape-ruled world; new twist ending). $100M budget; **$68.5M opening (higher than the reboot's)**; $180M domestic; $362M WW; 42% RT; CinemaScore B−; Fox abandoned the sequel despite profit; Burton: "I'd rather jump out a window." [HIGH; §1.1]
2. Rise of the Planet of the Apes (2011) — REBOOT / origin story, explicitly "not a continuation of the other films… an original story… a reinvention"; $93M budget; $54.8M opening (−20% vs the remake); $176.8M domestic (equal); $471–482M WW (+30%); 82% RT; CinemaScore A−; sequel greenlit within 10 months (announced 2012-05-31); the branch ran four films to 2024. [HIGH; §1.1]
3. Secondary pair — Ghostbusters (2016) REBOOT (alternate universe, same premise, all-new team, original cast in non-continuity cameos) vs Ghostbusters: Afterlife (2021) LEGACY SEQUEL (continuity kept, new leads, original cast in supporting continuity roles): identical openings ($46M / $44M) and near-identical worldwide ($229M / $203M); the reboot lost money on $144M, the legacy sequel profited on $75M and spawned a direct sequel. [HIGH; §1.5]

DESIGN INFERENCE: The remake drew a *larger* curiosity opening (the audience compares against a known film) but was judged against that film directly (B− CinemaScore, 42% RT) and left nothing to continue from — comparison pressure and redundancy showed up as weak legs and no branch. The reboot opened smaller but was judged on its own merits and produced a new bounded continuity that compounded. This matches owner direction I (remake = direct comparison; reboot = new bounded branch) and H (reboot useful when continuity is stale/dormant).

Safe rule: Remake: opening-reach bonus from the referenced film's Recognition, with a reception penalty/bonus scaled by comparison to that specific film's remembered quality; no new continuity branch is created. Reboot: starts a new branch with the StoryProperty's Recognition (discounted), Momentum reset to neutral, Fatigue reset partially; its own reception seeds the branch.

Must NOT over-generalize: A reboot is not a fatigue cure — Terminator Genisys (2015, soft reboot via retcon) and Dark Fate (2019, reset that erased T3–Genisys) both restarted continuity and both failed; restarting continuity cannot outrun accumulated distrust.

### G. How important is returning cast? (recast cases, and where a recast worked)

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Recast worked: Fury Road (Hardy for Gibson) — 97% RT, $380M WW, with the signature director present. Jurassic World (all-new leads) — record opening. Creed III (no Stallone) — franchise-record opening. Beneath (1970): Heston returned only in a brief bookend on condition his character die; the film still earned ~5–7x its budget. [HIGH; §1.3, §1.6, §1.2, §1.1]
2. Recast cited as a drag: Furiosa (Taylor-Joy for Theron, no Hardy) — trade press named the recast among the causes of the $31M 4-day opening vs $40–45M expectation. Space Jam: A New Legacy (LeBron for Jordan) — opening fine, 25% RT, Razzie Worst Actor. Terminator Genisys (Sarah/Kyle/John recast; Arnold back) — $89.8M domestic, 26% RT. [HIGH numbers / MEDIUM causal attribution; §1.3, §1.8, §1.4]
3. Returning legacy cast did NOT lift openings: Jurassic World Dominion (Neill/Dern/Goldblum) $145M vs Fallen Kingdom $148M; Terminator: Dark Fate (Hamilton + Schwarzenegger + Cameron producing) $29M opening, $120M+ loss; Expendables 3 (Gibson/Ford/Snipes/Banderas added) −44% opening. [HIGH; §1.6, §1.4, §1.7]
4. Returning lead *in support* strengthened a hand-off: Creed (Stallone, Oscar nom; film 95% RT); Ghostbusters: Afterlife (original four in supporting continuity roles; A− CinemaScore vs the 2016 reboot's B+ with the same actors as unrelated cameos). [HIGH; §1.2, §1.5]

DESIGN INFERENCE: Returning cast matters through *franchise-importance of the association* (owner direction F), not headcount. A signature director (Miller) or the StoryProperty itself carried recasts of the iconic lead; a recast of the character the spin-off is *named after*, with no bridging association, was penalized. Legacy cast returning to a fatigued or poorly-received film added nothing to opening reach; legacy cast in a *supporting* role inside a well-received hand-off film added reception and continuity value. The Ghostbusters 2016/Afterlife contrast shows the same actors are worth more as continuity roles than as non-continuity cameos.

Safe rule: Continuity value = importance-weight of the association × (present / recast / absent) applied to expectation and marketing efficiency, capped so that no single supporting association can dominate; returning iconic lead in a reduced role should still count as "present" for continuity purposes.

Must NOT over-generalize: No case supports mandatory cast lock-in; the largest openings in this set (Jurassic World, Creed III) came without the original leads. And returning cast never rescued a fatigued property (Dark Fate, Dominion). The sim must not let "all original cast back" override negative Momentum/Fatigue.

### H. How do ensemble/crossover casts change audience excitement? Fatigue after?

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. The Expendables (2010) opened $34.8M — ~1.9x Stallone's solo Rambo (2008, $18.2M) and Rambo: Last Blood (2019, $19.0M). The ensemble-of-fame roughly doubled the lead's solo opening. [HIGH; §1.7]
2. Adding more famous names did not sustain it: E2 (+Van Damme, Norris) −18% opening but +16% WW; E3 (+Gibson, Ford, Snipes, Banderas, Grammer, Rousey; PG-13; pre-release leak) −44% opening, −33% WW; Expend4bles (9 years later) $8.0M / $51M WW. Novelty decayed at 2-year cadence, and the pile-on of names was no substitute for the concept's freshness. [HIGH; §1.7; leak causality MEDIUM]
3. Cross-check (outside set; later-scope per owner O): The Avengers (2012) $207.4M vs component solo openings $65–128M (1.6–3.2x); the next solo entry rose +36% (Iron Man 3, $174.1M) — a crossover that was well received lifted its components rather than fatiguing them. [HIGH; §1.7]
4. Ghostbusters 2016's five original-cast cameos (as unrelated characters) did not lift its opening above what Afterlife later did with none of the reboot's cast and half the budget. [HIGH; §1.5]

DESIGN INFERENCE: An ensemble of famous associations raises the opening (attention) roughly in proportion to the *distinct* Recognition each brings, with diminishing returns; it does not raise reception and does not protect against fatigue. Whether fatigue follows depends on whether the ensemble was a *concept* (Expendables: "all the 80s action stars" — a one-time novelty) or a *world* (MCU: characters with their own momentum streams). In the Expendables case, each sequel's additions were lower-Recognition-per-name and the concept's novelty was already spent.

Safe rule: Ensemble/crossover attention = sum of association Recognition with concave saturation (the existing starDraw Hill-saturation on opening reach already encodes this shape); no reception bonus; sequels to a novelty-ensemble accrue Fatigue faster than sequels to a character-driven hit.

Must NOT over-generalize: Do not treat "more famous names" as monotonic — E3 had the most names and the biggest collapse. And do not derive crossover rules from Expendables alone; the MCU counter-case (crossover lifting solo entries) is the reason owner direction O treats crossovers as a separate, later design.

### I. How can celebrity cameo create attention without determining quality?

OBSERVED HISTORY:
1. Attention without quality: Space Jam (1996) — film born from Nike ads, A− CinemaScore, 46% RT, $27.5M opening, modest theatrical ($90.5M domestic on $80M) but 6x-platinum soundtrack and multi-billion merchandise. Space Jam: A New Legacy (2021) — opening beat forecasts by 50% ($31.1M vs $20M), 25% RT, Razzie Worst Actor, A− CinemaScore. [HIGH; §1.8]
2. Fame as LEAD without skill or fit: Kazaam (1996) — $5.0M opening, $18.9M domestic on $20M, 5% RT; Siskel: "marketing, not moviemaking." [HIGH; §1.8]
3. Fame as LEAD with skill and fit: 8 Mile (2002) — $51.2M opening (then second-highest R-rated ever), 76% RT, Oscar for the song; A Star Is Born (2018) — $42.9M opening, 90% RT, Best Actress nomination, best-selling soundtrack. In both, the musician played close to their own persona under a strong director. [HIGH; §1.8]
4. Fame in SUPPORTING/FEATURED/CAMEO: Trainwreck (LeBron, supporting, 84% RT) — press attention, praised performance, film's draw attributed to its lead/director; Dunkirk (Styles, featured) — director cast on merit and "wasn't aware" of the fame; the fame generated fan attention independent of the performance; Airplane!/Mary/Hangover athlete cameos — roles written for "an athlete," filled by the third choice or the available one; Tyson's cameo benefited Tyson's career more than it defined the film. [HIGH; §1.8]

DESIGN INFERENCE: Fame delivers *opening attention* (awareness/reach) in every role class; it delivers *quality* only through acting skill and role fit, which are separate attributes. The downside of fame-without-skill scales with role class: catastrophic in LEAD (Kazaam), invisible in CAMEO (Favre/Kareem interchangeable), and even positive in SUPPORTING when the performer is used for what they are (LeBron as LeBron). The cameo slot's value is largely fungible — a "recognizable athlete" slot, not a specific person — which naturally bounds its salary leverage. No case in this set shows multiple cameos rescuing a weak film (Ghostbusters 2016's five; Expendables 3's pile-on).

Safe rule: A CAMEO/FEATURED slot adds fame-weighted attention to opening reach at a small weight with saturation, contributes ~zero to reception, and carries ~zero reputational downside for the film; a famous non-actor in a PRINCIPAL slot contributes full fame to reach but exposes reception to their (low) acting skill at full CAST_WEIGHT. Cameo spam is prevented by saturation plus opportunity cost, not by a hard cap.

Must NOT over-generalize: Musicians are not "non-actors" as a class — Eminem and Gaga were Oscar-recognized; the sim must key on the person's acting skill attribute, not their fame source. And attention from fame decays fast (Space Jam 2's $31M opening → $70.6M domestic); fame should not lengthen legs.

## 4. Data caveats and open gaps

1. 1968–1973 Apes budgets differ across three sources (franchise article, individual film articles, The Numbers); presented as ranges. Domestic figures for that era mix rentals and gross and may include re-releases (the 1968 $32.6M vs "~$22M initial"). Percent declines are therefore approximate; the direction and monotonicity are not in doubt.
2. Loss estimates (Ghostbusters 2016 "$70M", Dark Fate "$120M+/$122.6M", Furiosa "$119.6M") are trade-press models (THR/Deadline) relayed by Wikipedia or ScreenRant, not audited statements; MEDIUM confidence throughout.
3. Verification pass 2026-09-12: every RT score previously marked "(memory, unverified)" was re-fetched from Wikipedia film/franchise articles or the RT page itself (Apes 1968 86, Dawn 91, War 94, Terminator 90/90, Ghostbusters 1984 95, Jurassic Park 91, JP III 49, Jurassic World 72 — corrected from 71, Fallen Kingdom 47). Two corrections resulted: The Terminator is 90% RT (not the 100% from memory) and Jurassic World is 72% (not 71%); neither changes any inference. The single remaining unverified score is The Lost World (54%, RT page 404). New detail surfaced: all four post-T2 Terminator films earned the same B+ CinemaScore, supporting the §1.4 reading that opening-weekend audiences kept showing up and rating alike while legs collapsed.
4. Stallone solo comparators now fully sourced from The Numbers (2026-09-12): Rambo (2008) $18.2M / $42.8M / $113.2M WW; Last Blood (2019) $18.9M / $44.8M / $91.4M WW. The ensemble-vs-solo opening ratio (1.9x) stands; on worldwide gross the ratio is 2.4x (Expendables $268M vs Rambo $113M).
5. Causal attributions for Furiosa (recast, demographics, streaming) and Expendables 3 (leak vs quality vs PG-13) are contested in the trade press; presented as reported, not established.
6. Inflation: all "record"/"outgrew" statements are nominal unless flagged; Creed III vs Rocky IV is flagged.
7. Not covered (out of Set B scope or not found): audience-tracking/marketing-spend data that would separate "recognition" from "marketing efficiency" in the revival openings; per-territory splits for Genisys/Terminator (China-led worldwide vs collapsing domestic) beyond the aggregate figures.
