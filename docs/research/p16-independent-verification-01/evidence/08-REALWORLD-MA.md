### A. Healthy strategic acquisitions

**F1. Disney/Pixar (2006) — all-stock, $7.4B, the target kept its name, site, HR policy and separate slate; the buyer already owned the films and sequel rights.**
- Disney press release, 2006-01-24: "2.3 Disney shares will be issued for each Pixar share. Based on Pixar's fully diluted shares outstanding, the transaction value is $7.4 billion"; "Both Disney and Pixar animation units will retain their current operations and locations."; "Pixar is currently in production on the final film under that agreement, Cars, to be distributed by Disney on June 9."
- Wikipedia *Pixar* §"Collaboration with Disney (1999–2006)": "Disney exclusively owned all story, character, and sequel rights and also collected a 10–15% distribution fee."; §"Walt Disney Studios subsidiary (2006–present)": "Some of those conditions were that Pixar HR policies would remain intact, including the lack of employment contracts. Also, the Pixar name was guaranteed to continue, and the studio would remain in its current Emeryville, California, location with the 'Pixar' sign."; "Catmull later explained that after the merger, to maintain the studios' separate identities and cultures...he and Lasseter 'drew a hard line' that each studio was solely responsible for its own projects."
- Wikipedia *Circle 7 Animation*: "In March 2004, Disney Circle Seven Animation was formed as a CGI animation studio to create sequels to the Disney-owned Pixar properties."; "On May 26, 2006, Disney shut down Circle Seven Animation, and transferred about 136 out of the studio's 168 employees to Walt Disney Feature Animation."
- NBC News 2006-01-24: "The two companies will remain separate, with Pixar keeping its brand name and headquarters in Emeryville"; Iger: "Animation has always been the heart and soul of the Walt Disney Company". Premium (search-snippet from the same NBC article): 2.3 × $25.99 = $59.78, "a 4 percent premium over Pixar's closing price of $57.57".
- **Proves:** (a) the buyer wanted *people and culture*, not the library it already owned; (b) film ownership + sequel rights can sit with one company while the creative studio is another — and the owner of sequel rights can (threaten to) make sequels without the creator (Circle 7); (c) the acquisition premium over market can be small when the price already reflects the deal; (d) an "autonomous subsidiary" model (assignment Model B) was chosen deliberately and enforced by contract.
- **Confidence:** HIGH (press release + Wikipedia + NBC). Premium figure MEDIUM (snippet).
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Bears on assignment §2.E Model B: real Model B exists and worked, but it was chosen because the *talent* was the asset.
- **FUN principle (inference):** "You are buying the people, not the shelf." A target whose value is a creative team is worth more *only if the team stays*; if the buyer already holds the films and sequel rights, the acquisition is a talent purchase and should be priced and risked as one.

**F2. Disney/Marvel (2009) — $4B, ~29% premium, 5,000 characters; existing third-party film licences survived the deal and had to be bought out or waited out; the subsidiary was progressively absorbed (2015, 2023).**
- Disney press release 2009-08-31: "Marvel shareholders would receive a total of $30 per share in cash plus approximately 0.745 Disney shares"; "the transaction value is $50 per Marvel share or approximately $4 billion"; "Disney will acquire ownership of Marvel including its more than 5,000 Marvel characters"; "Mr. Perlmutter will oversee the Marvel properties, and will work directly with Disney's global lines of business".
- NBC News 2009-08-31: "it offers shareholders a 29 percent premium to Friday's closing price."; "Paramount Pictures said in a statement its five-picture distribution deal with Marvel remains in force."; "Sony Pictures, the division of Sony Co behind the 'Spider-Man' movies, can continue to make movies in the franchise, under an existing agreement."; Iger: "The goal here is not to rebrand Marvel."
- Wikipedia *Marvel Studios* §"Acquisition by Disney and immediate changes (2009–2015)": "Walt Disney Studios Motion Pictures acquired the distribution rights for The Avengers (2012) and Iron Man 3 (2013) from Paramount Pictures"; "Disney purchased the distribution rights to Iron Man, Iron Man 2, Thor, and Captain America: The First Avenger from Paramount."; §"Restructuring under Walt Disney Studios (2015–2023)": "Marvel Studios was placed into Walt Disney Studios, with Feige reporting directly to Walt Disney Studios chairman Alan F. Horn instead of Perlmutter."
- Wikipedia *Marvel Entertainment* §"Disney subsidiary (2009–2023)": "Shareholders from both companies approved the deal on December 31, 2009; it was finalised on the same day."; "On March 29, 2023, Marvel Entertainment's remaining units were folded into Disney's other divisions."
- **Proves:** (a) buying the company does NOT unwind pre-existing licences of its properties — the buyer inherits the encumbrance and must pay again (Paramount buy-out) or wait for reversion (Fox, see F13); (b) a control premium near 30% for a healthy, scarce-IP target; (c) "retain brand as label/subsidiary" decays toward absorption over ~6–14 years.
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Directly supports assignment §2.B (a licence is a distinct right that survives a change of owner) and §2.E (label decay).
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Encumbered IP is discounted IP." Show the player, before buying, which of the target's Story Properties are already licensed out and when they come back; the deal price should visibly reflect it.

**F3. Disney/Lucasfilm (2012) — $4.05B half cash/half stock; buyer got the property, ILM, Skywalker Sound and the operating businesses; the seller's own sequel story treatments were discarded; the *original film's* distribution stayed with a third party in perpetuity.**
- Lucasfilm press release (SEC EX-99.1, 2012-10-30): "the transaction value is $4.05 billion, with Disney paying approximately half of the consideration in cash and issuing approximately 40 million shares"; "ownership of Lucasfilm ... including its massively popular and "evergreen" Star Wars franchise and its operating businesses in live action film production, consumer products, animation, visual effects, and audio post production"; "Kathleen Kennedy, current Co-Chairman of Lucasfilm, will become President of Lucasfilm, reporting to Walt Disney Studios Chairman Alan Horn."; "with George Lucas serving as creative consultant"; "Star Wars Episode 7 is targeted for release in 2015".
- Wikipedia *Star Wars sequel trilogy*: "In 2015, Lucas revealed (to his disappointment) that his outlines had been discarded in order to 'make something for the fans'."; combined sequel-trilogy gross "$4.477 billion".
- Wikipedia *Lucasfilm* §"Acquisition process" (paraphrase by fetch tool, corroborated below): Fox "were to retain the physical and theatrical distribution rights to both the prequel trilogy and Episodes V and VI...until May 2020, along with full distribution rights for the original 1977 film in perpetuity"; Paramount "would retain the distribution rights to the first four [Indiana Jones] films."; LucasArts: "On April 3, 2013, LucasArts...underwent closure, resulting in layoffs for most of its staff."
- SlashFilm (citing THR): "The original film, Star Wars Episode IV: A New Hope, is theirs forever" / Fox holds it "in perpetuity in all media worldwide" and "Episodes I-III, V and VI through May 2020."
- **Proves:** (a) a whole-company purchase can transfer the underlying property and future-continuation authority while a *specific film's* distribution right stays with someone else — even forever; (b) buying a company does not buy the founder's future creative direction; (c) a games unit was closed within six months (selective absorption inside a "retained" subsidiary).
- **Confidence:** HIGH for terms/roles (SEC filing); MEDIUM for the Fox rights detail (trade-press paraphrase; Wikipedia body was summarised by the fetch tool rather than quoted).
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Confirms assignment §2.B distinction "film/library ownership" ≠ "underlying Story Property ownership" ≠ "sequel authority".
- **FUN principle (inference):** "The property is not the print." A game rights bundle needs at least: Story Property (Disney), individual film rights (Fox for Ep IV), continuation authority (Disney). The player should be able to own the franchise while a rival still owns one old film in it.

**F4. Disney/21st Century Fox (2019) — $71.3B after a rival bid; the buyer wanted the *library* more than the production capacity; operations were absorbed, the studio name kept as a label with "Fox" removed; ~4,000 layoffs targeted; the inherited slate lost money.**
- Wikipedia *Acquisition of 21st Century Fox by Disney*: "Disney was to acquire Fox for $52.4 billion in stock (or 0.2745 Disney shares per 21CF share)"; "Comcast mounted an all-cash bid of $65 billion ($35 per share and a 19% premium over Disney's offer)"; "Disney and Fox announced they had amended their previous merger agreement, upping Disney's offer to $71.3 billion"; "It was less interested in 21st Century Fox's production capacities and more keen to acquire 21st Century Fox's own film and television libraries"; acquired "20th Century Fox...Fox Searchlight Pictures...20th Century Fox Television...FX Networks...a 73% stake in National Geographic Partners...a 30% stake in Hulu...Star India"; spun off to Fox Corporation: "Fox Broadcasting Company, Fox Television Stations...Fox News...Fox Business...Fox Sports...the Fox Studio Lot"; "20th Century Fox (rebranded to 20th Century Studios in January 2020), Fox Searchlight Pictures (shortened to Searchlight Pictures that same month)"; "Fox 2000 Pictures (which was shut down on May 14, 2021)".
- Deseret News 2020-01-21: "Walt Disney Company changed the name of 20th Century Fox to 20th Century Studios and Fox Searchlight Pictures to Searchlight Pictures."; "Walt Disney Company bought Fox last year for $71.3 billion, per The New York Times."
- Fortune 2019-03-20: "The Hollywood Reporter said last month that 4,000 jobs will be lost."; "Chief Executive Officer Bob Iger has promised $2 billion in cost savings, a commitment that all but assures epic job cuts."
- Wikipedia *Fox 2000 Pictures*: "Disney originally announced that Fox 2000 Pictures would continue operating after the acquisition, which gave Disney 11 film units. However, the next day, The Hollywood Reporter reported that Disney would shut down the studio in October 2019 after The Woman in the Window."
- CBR 2019-08-06 (Disney fiscal Q3 2019 call): "the studio posted a $170 million operating loss in its fiscal third quarter"; "the final X-Men film inherited from Fox partly led to the loss"; "the studio will now be axing a majority of the previously announced Fox slate and 'refocusing output'". Iger quote "well below where it had been and well below where we hoped it would be when we made the acquisition" — search-snippet only (Variety/Kidscreen gated).
- Film Stories: "Disney flogged the project to Netflix." (The Woman in the Window); "Disney selling the film to Amazon." (Everybody's Talking About Jamie); "Disney has now opted against a cinema release. It'll go to Hulu in the US and Amazon Prime elsewhere." (Deep Water).
- PBS NewsHour 2018-06-13: "Fox went with Disney because of concerns it would face more regulatory scrutiny with the other company."; "Comcast says its cash bid is 19 percent higher than the value of Disney offer as of Wednesday."; "It also agreed to reimburse Fox for the $1.5 billion-plus break-up fee it agreed to pay to Disney if their deal doesn't go through."
- **Proves:** (a) assignment Model C in the real world: operations absorbed, brand kept as a label (and even renamed to drop a word the buyer did not own); (b) the physical lot did NOT transfer (Fox Corp kept it); (c) inherited in-progress projects transfer and are finished, sold off, or written down — they are a liability as often as an asset; (d) a target board can prefer a *lower* bid for certainty; (e) competing bidders raise the price ~36% ($52.4B → $71.3B).
- **Confidence:** HIGH for price/rename/spin-off; MEDIUM for 4,000 (a THR estimate quoted by Fortune) and for the $170M loss (CBR reporting the earnings call).
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Confirms assignment §2.F "physical upgrades do NOT teleport" and §2.H "buyer chooses which to continue/cancel" as real patterns.
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Absorb, rename, and eat the slate." The label survives as a name on posters; the people mostly do not; the inherited slate should be a visible gamble with real downside (write-offs), not free revenue.

**F5. Amazon/MGM (2021–22) — $8.45B including $2.5B assumed debt; the stated value was the IP catalogue; purchase-price allocation put only $3.4B on the library and $4.9B on goodwill; the brand survived as a label; operations merged in 2023; the crown-jewel franchise came with creative control held by an outside partner until a separate 2025 deal.**
- Amazon press release 2021-05-26: "Amazon will acquire MGM for a purchase price of $8.45 billion."; Mike Hopkins: "The real financial value behind this deal is the treasure trove of IP in the deep catalog that we plan to reimagine and develop together with MGM's talented team."; "MGM has a vast catalog with more than 4,000 films ... as well as 17,000 TV shows"; Kevin Ulrich: "I am very proud that MGM's Lion, which has long evoked the Golden Age of Hollywood, will continue its storied history".
- Amazon Form 10-Q (quarter ended 2022-06-30), Note 1 "Acquisition Activity": "On March 17, 2022, we acquired MGM Holdings Inc. ("MGM"), for cash consideration of approximately $6.1 billion, net of cash acquired"; "We also assumed $2.5 billion of debt, which we repaid immediately after closing."; "The acquired assets primarily consist of $3.4 billion of video content and $4.9 billion of goodwill"; "The valuation of certain assets and liabilities is preliminary and subject to change."
- Wikipedia *Amazon MGM Studios* §"Acquisition of MGM and Rebranding (2021–present)": "On October 4, 2023 ... Amazon Studios would merge with MGM Holdings and would be renamed to Amazon MGM Studios"; "In November 2022, it was announced that Jennifer Salke, in addition to Amazon Studios, will be given full control of MGM's film and television divisions."; "continuing to operate as a label alongside Amazon Studios and Amazon Prime Video."; "In February 2025, Amazon MGM and Michael G. Wilson and Barbara Broccoli of Eon Productions announced its intentions to form a joint venture to manage the franchise rights to James Bond."; "Following the closing of the transaction, Amazon MGM would have full creative control over the Bond film franchise."; "In January 2024, Amazon announced hundreds of layoffs across Amazon MGM Studios, Prime Video and Twitch."
- Next TV: "The purchase price represents a 41% premium over earlier valuations made by Apple and Comcast"; "Amazon will now control only 50% of the Bond franchise—the duo of Barbara Broccoli and her half brother, Michael G. Wilson...controls the other half."
- **Proves:** (a) *whole-company* purchase transfers debt (assumed then repaid) — the headline price includes it; (b) the accounting split is the cleanest real-world demonstration that "library value" ≠ "transaction price": $3.4B content vs $4.9B goodwill (brand, franchise, relationships, team); (c) a control premium of roughly 40% over prior valuations for scarce IP; (d) label survives, operations merge within ~18 months, layoffs follow; (e) the most valuable Story Property came with a *shared creative-control encumbrance* that cost a second transaction to clear.
- **Confidence:** HIGH (press release + 10-Q + Wikipedia); premium MEDIUM (trade estimate).
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Directly bears on assignment §2.N (no double counting: library value and "brand/strategic" value are separate line items in real accounting).
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Two numbers on the deal sheet, never one." Show *library value* and *brand/strategic value* as separate visible components of a target's valuation; the premium attaches to the second.

**F6. Disney/Miramax (1993) — small cash price plus assumed debt; founders stayed in charge (Model B), then left (2005) and the name + library stayed with the buyer; the buyer later shut the unit and sold name + library (see F17).**
- Wikipedia *Miramax* §"Disney era (1993–2010)": "On June 30, 1993, The Walt Disney Company purchased Miramax Films for $60 million and assumed all of the company's debt, which was more than $40 million."; "Harvey and Bob Weinstein continued to operate Miramax Films until they left the company on September 30, 2005."; "The Miramax Films name and library remained with the film studio owned by Disney. The Weinsteins started a new film production company called The Weinstein Company (TWC) and took the Dimension Films label with them."; "The company's move of operations to The Walt Disney Studios in Burbank was completed on January 28, 2010, shutting down Miramax Films' separate New York and Los Angeles offices."
- **Proves:** debt transferred in a healthy purchase; brand and library stay with the acquiring owner when founders leave; a sub-label (Dimension) can be carved out and taken by departing talent; an autonomous subsidiary was eventually absorbed (2010) and then sold as a library-plus-name.
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Prior prose:** NEW.
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Labels are portable, people are not." A label can be owned, carved out, or sold; the founders' departure is the real event that changes a label from a studio into a shelf.

**F7. Viacom-Paramount/DreamWorks SKG (2005–06) — ~$1.6B including ~$400M debt; the buyer immediately sold a controlling stake in the *library* to financiers (Soros/Dune) for ~$900M while keeping distribution rights and 49%, then bought it back four years later for ~$400M.**
- Wikipedia *DreamWorks Pictures* §"Paramount ownership (2005–2008)": Viacom "agreed to purchase the live-action studio" for "approximately $1.6 billion, an amount that included about $400 million in debt assumptions."; "completed ... on February 1, 2006."; "Viacom agreed to sell a controlling interest in the 1997–2005 DreamWorks Pictures live-action library to Soros Strategic Partners and Dune Entertainment II."; "The film library (consisting of 59 titles) was valued at $900 million" with Viacom retaining "a minority 49% stake." "Paramount Pictures retained the worldwide distribution rights."; "On February 8, 2010, Viacom repurchased Soros' controlling stake in the DreamWorks Pictures library for around $400 million."; §"Reliance-Spielberg joint venture (2008–2015)": "On September 22, 2008, it was announced that DreamWorks closed a deal with Indian investment firm Reliance Anil Dhirubhai Ambani Group to create a $1.2 billion stand-alone production company and end its ties with Paramount."
- **Proves:** the operating studio, the library, the distribution right to that library, and the brand are four separately tradable things — the library alone fetched more than half the whole-company price, then came back cheaper; the brand left with the founders.
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Strongest real evidence for assignment §2.D "buy and sell ... film/library rights" as a distinct transaction from studio acquisition.
- **FUN principle (inference):** "The shelf can be sold without selling the shop." Library rights as a sellable asset class create a recovery lever (assignment §2.K) that is historically real, and a buy-back is a legitimate later move.

**F8. Orion/Filmways (1982) and Warner/Lorimar-Telepictures (1988–89) — two absorptions where the library was the motive and the acquired label was kept for a few years then consolidated "for economic reasons"; the studio lot was sold separately.**
- FundingUniverse *Orion Pictures Corporation* history: "In February 1982 Orion announced that it would take over the company. Orion's partners in the $26 million deal to purchase Filmways were E. M. Warburg Pincus & Company ... and Home Box Office, Inc. (HBO)"; Filmways had "a library of 500 films as well as its distribution operation".
- Wikipedia *Orion Pictures* §"1982–1986": "Orion subsequently purchased Filmways and reorganized the flailing company."; "Its syndicated subsidiary Filmways Enterprises, was renamed to Orion Entertainment Corporation, and its television division was renamed to Orion Television."
- Wikipedia *Lorimar Television* §"Purchase by Warner Communications and consolidation with Warner Bros. Television (1987–1993)": "In June 1988, Lorimar was purchased by Warner Communications"; "Lorimar continued as a production company until September 1993, when it was eventually consolidated into Warner Bros. Television, primarily for economic reasons."; "In 1986 they purchased the Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer (MGM) studio lot in Culver City... The former MGM studio lot was sold to Sony to house Columbia Pictures, TriStar Pictures... towards the end of 1989"; §"Theatrical films": "In 1988 ... Lorimar struck a new distribution deal with Warner Bros. This deal effectively ended Lorimar's in-house distribution wing."; "Warner Bros. now owns most of Lorimar's catalogue".
- **Proves:** absorption with label retention has a natural half-life (~4–5 years here); the real estate is disposed of separately; the library is what persists under the new owner.
- **Confidence:** HIGH (Filmways price), MEDIUM-HIGH (Lorimar detail — Wikipedia only).
- **Prior prose:** NEW.
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Labels fade unless fed." If a retained label has no separately managed operations (Owner direction), it should be presented as a *heritage tag* on releases and history, with an optional player choice to retire it — not as a second studio.

### B. Distressed / bankruptcy asset sales

**F9. The Weinstein Company (2018) — Ch.11 §363 sale to a stalking-horse bidder with no competing bids; price cut before closing; buyer assumed a defined slice of project liabilities and took the library free and clear of the rest; unpaid talent participations stayed behind as claims against the estate.**
- Cravath deal note: "On May 8, 2018, Judge Mary F. Walrath...approved the sale"; "$310 million in cash plus the assumption of up to $127.5 million in liabilities"; "the company entered into a 'stalking horse' agreement with an affiliate of Lantern"; "Lantern agreed to purchase substantially all of the assets of TWC".
- Gulf News/AP: "Lantern ... won the assets by default last week when Weinstein Co.'s planned bankruptcy auction attracted no serious competitive bids by the April 30 deadline."; "$310 million ... in cash and will assume about $115 million in liabilities related to film and television projects."
- Fortune 2018-07-06: "$289 million after the Weinstein Co.'s unsecured creditors objected to the company lowering its price from $310 million to $287 million."
- TheWrap 2018-07: "a $289 million sale out of bankruptcy to Lantern Capital"; approved "despite objections from high-profile filmmakers like Quentin Tarantino, who are concerned about unpaid film royalties."; "What was once a 140-person staff has been reduced to roughly 50."; Lantern offered "contracts to roughly two-thirds of TWC's former employees."
- Wikipedia *The Weinstein Company*: "On March 19, 2018, the Weinstein Company announced that it had declared bankruptcy, and is considering a $310 million stalking horse offer with Lantern Capital."; "On January 7, 2019, Lantern Entertainment said that it was not responsible for TWC's unpaid debts."; "On March 20, 2020, a federal judge ruled that Spyglass was not responsible for any of TWC's outgoing royalties."
- Wikipedia *Lantern Entertainment*: "Lantern Entertainment was formed and assumed the rights to TWC's 277-film library."; "In November 2018, Lantern acquired full control of three Quentin Tarantino films (Inglourious Basterds, Django Unchained and The Hateful Eight), originally released by The Weinstein Company, for $5.5 million."; "On March 13, 2019, Lantern and Gary Barber relaunched Spyglass Media Group, which would absorb all of TWC and Lantern Entertainment's assets into the new company."
- Jones Day memo on *In re Weinstein Co. Holdings*, 997 F.3d 497 (3d Cir. 2021): "section 363(f) permits the rights and obligations under one non-executory contract to be transferred free and clear of claims"; "At the time of the bankruptcy filing, Cohen was owed approximately $400,000 in profits from the film."; the Third Circuit affirmed that the buyer acquired the film without triggering cure obligations (search-summary of multiple law-firm memos: "since it was not executory, Spyglass would only be responsible for obligations that arose after the date the sale closed").
- **Proves:** (a) the distressed price is a *floor set by a stalking horse* and can move DOWN before closing when no rival appears; (b) the buyer chooses which liabilities to assume (a capped bucket tied to projects it wants) and leaves the rest — including the founder-era tort exposure — with the estate; (c) talent's unpaid participations on *old films* can be stranded, which is exactly why talent objected; (d) staff transfer is by fresh offer, not automatic (two-thirds offered contracts); (e) the buyer separately bought "full control" of three specific films afterwards — an individual film-rights transaction distinct from the library purchase.
- **Confidence:** HIGH.
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Confirms assignment §2.M option 3 ("clean asset purchase — creditors settle old debt; buyer acquires chosen assets without liabilities") as the real default, QUALIFIED by the fact that real buyers still assume a *chosen, capped* set of project obligations (i.e., real deals are the assignment's HYBRID option 5).
- **FUN principle (inference):** "Pick your lots, leave the ghosts." In a bankruptcy purchase the buyer selects assets and any contracts it wants to keep (paying their cure), and everything else stays with the dead studio — including its debts and any promises it made to talent on films the buyer takes. The stranded parties remember.

### D. §363 bankruptcy asset sales — background only (never a simulator)

**F22. Statutory mechanics.**
- 11 U.S.C. §363(b)(1): "The trustee, after notice and a hearing, may use, sell, or lease, other than in the ordinary course of business, property of the estate".
- §363(f): property may be sold "free and clear of any interest" if one of five conditions holds (non-bankruptcy law permits; the interest-holder consents; the interest is a lien and the price exceeds all liens; the interest is in bona fide dispute; the holder could be compelled to accept money).
- §363(k): "Unless the court for cause orders otherwise the holder of such claim may bid at such sale, and, if the holder of such claim purchases such property, such holder may offset such claim against the purchase price." (credit bidding)
- §365(a): the trustee may "assume or reject any executory contract or unexpired lease of the debtor"; §365(b)(1): assumption of a defaulted contract requires cure (or adequate assurance of prompt cure), compensation for pecuniary loss, and adequate assurance of future performance; §365(f)(2): assignment only if assumed and "adequate assurance of future performance by the assignee"; §365(g): rejection "constitutes a breach" (a pre-petition claim).
- Wikipedia *Stalking horse offer*: "A stalking horse offer, agreement, or bid is a bid for a bankrupt firm or its assets that is arranged in advance of an auction to act, in effect, as a reserve bid."; purpose "to maximize the value of its assets or avoid low bids"; protections: "breakup fees", expense reimbursement, minimum overbid increments ("the next bid at least $20 million higher").
- **Design-level summary (this reviewer's paraphrase):** (1) the estate picks a stalking horse whose bid becomes the *floor* and who gets a break-up fee if outbid; (2) an auction is held; competing bidders must beat the floor by a set increment; secured lenders can bid with their debt (credit bid) instead of cash; if nobody bids, the stalking horse wins (TWC) — and may then *cut its price* before closing; (3) the buyer takes assets *free and clear* of old claims, which attach to the sale proceeds; (4) contracts the buyer wants are *assumed* (with arrears cured) and *assigned* to it; contracts it does not want are *rejected* and become unsecured claims against the estate; (5) non-executory obligations tied to an asset (e.g., unpaid back-end on an old film) may not travel with the asset at all (Cohen/Weinstein); (6) people are not "assumed" — they are offered new contracts (TWC: two-thirds).
- **Confidence:** HIGH (statute text + Wikipedia + F9).
- **Prior prose:** NEW. Confirms assignment §2.M options 1, 3, 4, 5 all correspond to real mechanisms; option 2 (whole-studio auction) corresponds to a plan sale/reorganisation (MGM 2010, Relativity 2018).

### E. Healthy-company control premiums — background only

**F23. Premium ranges and refusal behaviour.**
- Wikipedia *Control premium*: "A control premium is an amount that a buyer is sometimes willing to pay over the current market price of a publicly traded company in order to acquire a controlling share in that company."; "In business practice, control premiums may vary from 20% to 40%."; "the maximum value that an acquirer firm would be willing to pay should equal the sum of the target firm's intrinsic value, synergies that the acquiring firm can expect to achieve between the two firms, and the opportunity cost of not acquiring the target firm."; "actual premiums paid have varied widely".
- Observed in this dossier: Pixar ~4% over an already-run-up price (F1); Marvel 29% (F2); Comcast's Fox bid 19% over Disney's, final Disney price ~36% over its own first offer (F4); Amazon/MGM ~41% over prior strategic valuations (F5); Sony/MGM 2004 won a bidding war Time Warner "did not want" (F12).
- Refusal: Deseret News 2004-04-29 — Comcast's "2-month-old unsolicited bid" for Disney, "originally valued at $54 billion", was rejected "as too low"; Disney "promised to stay the course with its current management"; Comcast: "As a result, we have withdrawn our offer". PBS 2018: "Fox went with Disney because of concerns it would face more regulatory scrutiny with the other company" despite Comcast's cash bid being "19 percent higher".
- **Proves:** (a) 20–40% is the ordinary band; scarce strategic IP pushes to the top of it or above; (b) a healthy target's board can and does refuse regardless of price (Disney 2004) or accept a *lower* bid for certainty/fit (Fox 2018); (c) competing bidders, not the target's asking price, are what drive the premium up.
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (secondary finance source + case data).
- **Prior prose:** CONFIRMS assignment §2.L/§2.O premise ("A healthy studio should be allowed to reject an offer. Price may influence willingness strongly") and §2.N Future Ops view ("healthy acquisition begins from enterprise valuation + control premium, while bankruptcy auction price emerges from bids around liquidation/strategic value") — verified against F9/F11/F13 on the auction side.

## 4. Cross-case synthesis

### 4.1 What actually transferred (whole-company deals)

| Element | Healthy purchase (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, Fox, MGM/Amazon, Miramax '93, DreamWorks) | §363 / distressed (TWC, Carolco, Relativity, Orion '92 plan, MGM 2010 prepack) |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | Becomes buyer's cash; headline price is quoted "net of cash acquired" (Amazon 10-Q) | Estate cash goes to creditors, not the buyer |
| Debt / liabilities | Transfer with the entity; often repaid at close (MGM $2.5B; DreamWorks ~$400M; Miramax >$40M) | Stay with the estate except a *chosen, capped* assumed set (TWC "up to $127.5M ... related to film and television projects") |
| Employee contracts | Transfer intact; the buyer then restructures (Fox 4,000 target; LucasArts closed; Marvel folded 2023) | Not automatic: buyer offers new contracts (TWC two-thirds); rejected contracts = claims |
| Active productions / slate | Transfer and finish under buyer, who may cancel, sell or write down (Fox: $170M loss, films sold to Netflix/Amazon; Cars finished under old deal) | Frozen in the estate; sold as lots or released later by the reorganised entity (Orion's 10 films; Relativity's Masterminds; TWC's finished films "screened for buyers") |
| Story Properties / franchise rights | Transfer, *subject to* existing licences and partner encumbrances (Marvel: Sony/Fox/Universal/Paramount; MGM: Eon on Bond) | Sold as separate lots from the library (Carolco: library → Canal+, Terminator → Vajna) |
| Film library | Transfers; is the stated motive in Fox, MGM 2004/2021, Filmways, Orion/Goldwyn | The principal lot; free and clear; old participations may not travel (Cohen) |
| Equipment / facilities / land | Frequently *excluded or sold*: Fox lot stayed with Fox Corp; Lorimar/MGM lot → Sony; RKO lot → Desilu | Sold separately (RKO) |
| Technology / know-how | Transfers as operating businesses (ILM, Skywalker Sound) — but the *people* are the knowledge; no case shows a "facility teleport" | n/a in sampled cases |
| Brand / label | Kept as label or subsidiary, then decays: Marvel Ent. (14 yrs), Lorimar (5), Miramax (17, then sold), Orion (1), MGM label (label survives, ops merged in ~18 months); Pixar/Lucasfilm retained because talent+brand was the asset | Name can be sold *alone* (Carolco 2015; RKO trademark) |
| Founder's future creative direction | Does NOT transfer (Lucas treatments discarded; Lasseter later exited) | n/a |

### 4.2 Price formation

| Situation | Real mechanism | Observed numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy, willing seller | Enterprise value + control premium; competing bidders raise it | Pixar +4% (pre-priced); Marvel +29%; Fox $52.4B → $71.3B under a $65B rival bid; MGM ~+41% over prior strategic valuations |
| Healthy, unwilling seller | Board refuses; buyer withdraws or goes hostile; certainty/fit can beat price | Disney rejected Comcast 2004 "as too low"; Fox chose Disney over a 19%-higher Comcast bid on regulatory risk |
| Distressed, going concern | Prepackaged plan: debt-for-equity in weeks; no cash price at all | MGM 2010: >$4B debt → equity in ~30 days |
| Distressed, asset sale | Stalking-horse floor → auction → free-and-clear close; price can move down (no bidders) or up (rival) | TWC $310M → $289M (no bids); Carolco $47.5M (Fox) → $58M (Canal+); Relativity $250M lender credit bid as floor |
| Library alone | Priced by expected window cash flow; can exceed half the whole-company price | DreamWorks: 59 films at $900M vs $1.6B whole company; RKO TV rights $15.2M of $25M; Amazon: $3.4B content of $8.45B |

### 4.3 Brand fate patterns mapped to assignment §2.E models

| Assignment model | Real instances | Real outcome |

## 5. Design implications for P16 (this reviewer's inference — NOT approved scope)

Each item names the assignment section it informs. None reopens an Owner decision; where a selected rule is stress-tested, the smallest correction is stated.

1. **§2.B minimal rights bundle — four kinds are enough, and are all historically real:** (i) Story Property ownership (Marvel/Spider-Man; Disney/Star Wars); (ii) Film ownership per released film (Fox/Episode IV; StudioCanal/T2); (iii) Continuation authority — sequel/remake/spinoff as one right (Terminator; Circle 7; RKO remake rights); (iv) a Licence — time-limited or activity-conditioned grant of (ii) or (iii) with a visible reversion week (Sony/Spider-Man; Daredevil; Fox/Star Wars May 2020; Netflix/Miramax). Territories, media sub-splits and exclusivity flags are NOT needed for any of the observed decisions; P18 can add "television/cross-media" as a fifth kind later. *Inference.*

2. **§2.A — Story Property ≠ film is confirmed by every franchise case;** the Pixar/Circle 7 episode proves the owner of continuation rights can act *without the creator*, so the game's StoryProperty must record continuation authority separately from the studio that made the film. *Inference; CONFIRMS P12-TO-P13 handoff line 28 ("films/projects/credits do not imply StoryProperty, rights/license or continuation authority").*

3. **§2.C — library value is stored, not flowing:** RKO (1955 TV), MGM (2004 DVD), Miramax (2011 streaming), Amazon (2021 streaming) each show library *cash* appearing only when a downstream window exists. Recommend P16 carry a **Library Valuation** line (feeds enterprise value and auction lots) and *no* weekly library income until P18 defines a window. The Amazon 10-Q split ($3.4B content / $4.9B goodwill) is the model for keeping "library value" and "brand/strategic value" as two visible components. *Inference; CONFIRMS assignment §2.C and §2.N.*

4. **§2.E — Model D is the observed real norm;** but real "retained label" (Model C) never operates independently for long. Recommend: a Label is a *heritage tag* (name + logo + founding StudioId + acquisition event) attachable to future releases, with a player option to retire it; it owns nothing and manages nothing. This keeps Owner direction ("labels should not become a second separately managed studio"). Model B (Pixar) should be explicitly *not offered* — its real success depended on contractual talent protections the game does not model. *Inference.*

5. **§2.F — whole-company bundle answers, from the cases:** (1) acquired cash → buyer cash (net-of-cash pricing); (2) debt → transfers with the entity in a healthy purchase (MGM, DreamWorks, Miramax) — if P11 has no debt object, the transfer bundle must say "known obligations transfer as obligations"; (3) employee contracts → transfer intact (Fox) then ordinary P14 termination law applies (4,000 layoffs were ordinary terminations at cost); (5) active movies → transfer and finish, buyer may cancel = write-off (Fox $170M); (6) facilities → do NOT come to the buyer's lot in any case (Fox lot, RKO lot, MGM Culver lot all sold or retained by others) → liquidate at closing or hold as a financial asset (§2.G options 1/3); (7) research → knowledge transfers with the people/business (ILM), never as an installed facility; (8) higher physical upgrade at target → the buyer gains eligibility, not the building (no case shows otherwise). *Inference; CONFIRMS the assignment's "knowledge transfers, physical installations do not magically appear" principle.*

6. **§2.H — three real handling patterns, one per situation:** healthy purchase → projects transfer and finish under the buyer, buyer may cancel/sell (Fox); bankruptcy → each finished or in-progress film is a *separate auction lot* with its own reserve (Orion, Relativity, TWC "finished films screened for buyers"); reorganisation-in-place (MGM 2010) → projects continue under the same StudioId with new owners. No case requires a "transition owner" state beyond "in estate, unsold". *Inference; CONFIRMS assignment §2.H option 4.*

7. **§2.I — history model:** RKO, Orion, Lorimar, Miramax and TWC all show the *original* studio surviving as a dated historical entity (with credits intact) while its assets carry an ownership relation to the successor. The assignment's status strings (BANKRUPTCY / ACQUISITION / MERGER-ABSORPTION with successor owner) match reality; add "LIBRARY SOLD" and "LABEL RETIRED" as dated events only if the corresponding transactions exist. *Inference; CONFIRMS P15 line 539.*

8. **§2.L/§2.O — healthy premium model:** real behaviour is (a) a 20–40% band, top of band for scarce IP; (b) the target's *board* has a refusal right independent of price (Disney 2004); (c) a competing bidder, not the seller's ask, is what raises price (Fox, MGM 2004); (d) a lower bid can win on certainty. Recommend the player sees: enterprise valuation, a *visible asking-premium band* driven by target success + independence preference, and the existence (not identity) of any rival bidder. No hidden multiplier; the target can say no. Repeated rejected bids in reality harden the target (Disney "stay the course") — a simple "cooling-off" after refusal is consistent. *Inference; CONFIRMS assignment §2.L/§2.O premises.*

9. **§2.M — distressed acquisition:** real practice is the assignment's HYBRID: clean asset lots free and clear (option 3), *plus* an option for the buyer to assume specific project contracts at their cure cost (option 4), *plus* a lender/creditor floor (credit bid) that stops give-away prices (Relativity). Whole-entity purchase (option 2) is the *reorganisation* route (MGM 2010) and is the only route that keeps the StudioId operating. Recommend: asset lots by default; whole-entity only when the Owner wants "buy the studio and keep its label". *Inference; CONFIRMS §2.M option 5 as most realistic and, in this reviewer's view, most legible (each lot has a price, each assumed contract has a cure cost, everything else is the dead studio's problem).*

10. **§2.N — three numbers, verified:** book net worth (assets − liabilities: Relativity claimed assets $100–500M vs liabilities $500M–$1B, i.e., negative), enterprise valuation (Amazon's $8.45B = content + goodwill + working capital), transaction price (healthy: valuation + premium; distressed: floor → auction). Double-counting risk is precisely the goodwill line: if "brand" and "library" and "franchise" are all separately summed and *then* a premium is added, the price inflates twice. Recommend valuation = library value + Story Property value + tangible net assets + a *single* brand/strategic component; premium applies to the total once. *Inference.*

11. **§2.S — anti-snowball levers that are natural because they are real:** inherited-slate losses (Fox); integration cost (Disney's $2B savings target = layoffs at cost); talent departure risk (Lucas, Lasseter); encumbered IP (Marvel licences); rival bidders (Canal+, Comcast, Sony vs Time Warner); creditor floors (Relativity); leverage → foreclosure (Cannon/Parretti). None is an artificial cap. Antitrust appeared once as a *target-side* preference (Fox 2018) — consistent with the assignment's "only if absolutely necessary later". *Inference.*

12. **§2.K — selling player assets:** real precedents for each sellable category: library (DreamWorks/Soros, RKO/C&C), individual film rights (Lantern's three Tarantino films for $5.5M), continuation rights (Terminator, six times), brand/name (Carolco, RKO), development slate (Miramax 2010 "development projects"), lot (RKO/Desilu, MGM/Sony). Real anti-flip friction is price discovery — buy-backs happen at different prices (DreamWorks library sold at $900M, bought back at ~$400M). A simple rule "a sold asset cannot be repurchased by the seller for N weeks, and re-sale carries a visible discount" mirrors reality without a hidden formula. *Inference.*

13. **§2.J — rival M&A symmetry:** every observed price-raising event came from a rival bidder; giving rivals the same auction/offer law is not only fair but the main source of tension. AI should bid when a lot's *library or property value* exceeds its reserve and the AI's affordability, and should decline bidding wars past its own valuation (Time Warner 2004 behaviour). *Inference.*

**Smallest-correction flag (one):** the assignment's §2.F premise that "debt transfers" in a whole-company purchase is correct for *healthy* purchases but must be explicitly *false* for §363 asset purchases, or the two paths will produce the same "buy the studio" screen with silently different liability outcomes. Recommend the transaction record carry an explicit `liabilitiesAssumed` list (empty by default in an auction, full by default in a healthy purchase). *Inference.*

## 6. Open questions (for the design phase, not for the Owner unless flagged)

1. Does P16's first slice need the Licence right at all, or only Sale? The most legible real mechanic (use-it-or-lose-it reversion) needs a per-licence reversion week and a check on release — small, but it is a timer. (Design decision.)
2. Should Brand/Label be a *sellable* asset separate from library (Carolco/RKO precedent says yes) or only a heritage tag that travels with whole-company purchase? (Owner preference for no second studio suggests: tag only, sellable only with the library.)
3. Inherited-slate write-downs (Fox) require a P11 vocabulary for cancelling a transferred project at cost; is that already covered by existing project-cancellation law? (Not verified here — P11 dossier.)
4. Credit bidding requires a lender entity; P11 currently has no debt object (roadmap line 165–167). What stands in for the creditor floor in an auction — a fixed liquidation reserve? (Design decision; see F13.)
5. Continuation-rights value tracked the last film's performance in reality (Terminator $8M → $25M → $29.5M → <$20M). Should P16 price continuation rights from P07 outcomes, or leave that to P17? (Boundary question §2.T.)
6. Partner-encumbered properties (Bond/Eon; Hulk/Universal) — is a single "shared control / cannot continue until bought out" flag worth having in P16, or is that P17 scope? (Boundary question.)
